    6.1Mobile robot localization

    Localization: estimating pose relative to a given map

    Mobile robot localization (position estimation) is the problem of determining the pose of a robot relative to a given map. It is the most basic perceptual problem in mobile robotics: nearly every task, from fetching a tote to docking on a charger, needs the pose. The pose cannot be sensed directly; it must be inferred from data, so localization is a coordinate transformation problem between the map frame and the robot frame solved by filtering.

    A single range reading usually cannot determine the pose: many poses in a corridor produce the same reading. The robot must integrate data over time, which is exactly what the Bayes filter does.

    Three dimensions that characterise localization problems

    Review question 1 (ESA question 7a) asks for the dimensions that characterise localization problems. Thrun et al. describe the following; the fourth is often added:

    1. Local versus global (the initial knowledge and knowledge available at run time): position tracking, global localization, kidnapped robot.
    2. Static versus dynamic environments: whether objects other than the robot change state. Dynamic changes that persist (moved furniture) are harder than transient ones (people walking).
    3. Passive versus active: in passive localization the estimator only observes while the robot does its task; in active localization the robot chooses motions to reduce uncertainty, for example driving into a room corner rather than along a symmetric corridor.
    4. Single-robot versus multi-robot: robots that can detect each other can share beliefs and localize one another.

    Local versus global localization

    TLO2 and review question 2 ask you to distinguish these (ESA question 7b):

    CriterionPosition tracking (local)Global localizationKidnapped robot
    Initial poseKnown approximatelyUnknownBelieved known but wrong
    UncertaintySmall, localLarge, whole mapLarge, not represented

    Exam answer
    Local localization compensates small odometry errors around a known start. Global localization must determine the pose without an initial guess and therefore needs a belief that can hold several hypotheses. The kidnapped-robot problem is harder still, because the robot confidently believes a wrong pose and must recognise its own failure.

    6.3Estimating correspondences

    Which landmark did I see?

    Feature-based EKF localization needs the correspondence ctic_t^icti​: which map landmark generated feature iii. When landmarks have unique signatures (AprilTags) it is known; otherwise it must be estimated. The standard approach is maximum likelihood data association:

    c^t=arg⁡max⁡ctp(zt∣c1:t,m,z1:t−1,u1:t),\hat c_t = \arg\max_{c_t} p(z_t \mid c_{1:t}, m, z_{1:t-1}, u_{1:t}),c^t​=argct​max​p(zt​∣c1:t​,m,z1:t−1​,u1:t​),

    implemented per feature by computing, for every candidate landmark kkk, the predicted measurement z^k\hat z^kz^k, the innovation covariance SkS^kSk, and choosing the kkk with the largest Gaussian likelihood, equivalently the smallest Mahalanobis distance dk2=(z−z^k)T(Sk)−1(z−z^k)d_k^2 = (z - \hat z^k)^T (S^k)^{-1}(z - \hat z^k)dk2​=(z−z^k)T(Sk)−1(z−z^k).

    A validation gate rejects matches with d2d^2d2 above a chi-square threshold (for 2 degrees of freedom, 5.99 at 95 percent, 9.21 at 99 percent), and unmatched features are treated as clutter or new landmarks.

    When the gates overlap

    Why wrong associations are dangerous
    An EKF that commits to a wrong correspondence performs a confident update in the wrong direction, shrinks its covariance and then rejects the correct associations that follow. The filter diverges without knowing it. Remedies: use signatures, require mutual consistency of several features (joint compatibility branch and bound), delay the decision, or keep several hypotheses (next topic).

    6.4Multi-hypothesis tracking

    Keep several Gaussians alive

    Multi-hypothesis tracking (MHT) extends EKF localization to multimodal beliefs by representing the belief as a mixture of Gaussians:

    bel(xt)=1∑lψt,l∑lψt,l det⁡(2πΣt,l)−1/2exp⁡ ⁣(−12(xt−μt,l)TΣt,l−1(xt−μt,l)).bel(x_t) = \frac{1}{\sum_l \psi_{t,l}}\sum_l \psi_{t,l}\,\det(2\pi\Sigma_{t,l})^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(x_t - \mu_{t,l})^T\Sigma_{t,l}^{-1}(x_t - \mu_{t,l})\right).bel(xt​)=∑l​ψt,l​1​l∑​ψt,l​det(2πΣt,l​)−1/2exp(−21​(xt​−μt,l​)TΣt,l−1​(xt​−μt,l​)).

    Each component lll is an EKF that corresponds to one sequence of data-association decisions, and ψt,l\psi_{t,l}ψt,l​ is its weight, updated by the likelihood of that component's measurements. When a feature could belong to two landmarks, the filter branches into two tracks instead of choosing.

    Managing hypotheses

    The number of association histories grows exponentially, so MHT must prune. Practical rules:

    • Delete components whose weight falls below a threshold (for example 10−310^{-3}10−3 of the largest).
    • Merge components whose means are close in Mahalanobis distance.
    • Cap the number of components.

    MHT solves global localization when the environment has few, distinctive features and the number of plausible places is small. It is efficient, because each hypothesis is a compact Gaussian, but it inherits the EKF's linearisation errors and cannot represent arbitrary shapes.

    Real-life example
    Multi-hypothesis trackers are standard in radar and automotive perception for tracking many objects with ambiguous detections; the same ideas are used by self-driving stacks to track pedestrians and vehicles.

    6.5Grid localization, Monte Carlo localization

    Grid localization: a histogram filter over pose

    Grid localization applies the histogram filter to pose space. The belief is a collection of discrete probabilities bel(xt)={pk,t}bel(x_t) = \{p_{k,t}\}bel(xt​)={pk,t​} over a grid in (x,y,θ)(x, y, \theta)(x,y,θ). Each cycle predicts every cell with the motion model (a convolution) and multiplies by the measurement model evaluated at the cell centre. It solves global localization and the kidnapped robot problem because any belief shape is representable.

    Memory and resolution
    1. A 10 m x 6 m room at 5 cm with 5-degree heading bins: 200×120×72=1 728 000200 \times 120 \times 72 = 1\,728\,000200×120×72=1728000 cells, 6.9 MB in single precision.
    2. A 100 m x 60 m warehouse at the same resolution is 100 times larger: 173 million cells.
    3. Hence grid localizers use coarse grids (15-50 cm, 5-10 degrees), selective updates of only high-probability cells, or tree-based adaptive grids.
    Pitfall
    Coarse cells make the measurement model too peaked at the cell centre. A cell that contains the true pose may score poorly because its centre is 20 cm away. Smooth the measurement model, or evaluate it over the cell area.

    Monte Carlo localization

    Monte Carlo localization (MCL) is the particle filter applied to localization. Each particle is a pose hypothesis. Algorithm MCL(Xt−1,ut,zt,m)(\mathcal{X}_{t-1}, u_t, z_t, m)(Xt−1​,ut​,zt​,m):

    1. For m=1…Mm = 1\ldots Mm=1…M: xt[m]=sample_motion_model(ut,xt−1[m])x_t^{[m]} = \text{sample\_motion\_model}(u_t, x_{t-1}^{[m]})xt[m]​=sample_motion_model(ut​,xt−1[m]​); wt[m]=measurement_model(zt,xt[m],m)w_t^{[m]} = \text{measurement\_model}(z_t, x_t^{[m]}, m)wt[m]​=measurement_model(zt​,xt[m]​,m).
    2. Resample MMM particles with probability proportional to wt[m]w_t^{[m]}wt[m]​.

    Making MCL robust: random particles and adaptive sample size

    Plain MCL cannot recover from failure: once all particles have collapsed near a wrong pose, no particle exists near the truth. Augmented MCL injects random particles with probability max⁡(0,1−wfast/wslow)\max(0, 1 - w_{fast}/w_{slow})max(0,1−wfast​/wslow​), where wsloww_{slow}wslow​ and wfastw_{fast}wfast​ are long- and short-term averages of the measurement likelihood. When measurements suddenly fit badly (the robot was kidnapped), wfastw_{fast}wfast​ drops below wsloww_{slow}wslow​ and random particles appear.

    KLD sampling adapts the number of particles: many while the belief is spread (global localization), few once it has converged (position tracking), chosen so that the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the sample and the true posterior stays below a bound with high confidence.
