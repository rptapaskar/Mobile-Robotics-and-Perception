    3.1Robot environment interaction

    State: everything that matters for the future

    The state xtx_txt​ collects all aspects of the robot and its environment that can influence the future. Review question 1 asks for the typical state variables:

    • Robot pose: (x,y,θ)(x, y, \theta)(x,y,θ) in the plane, or six values in 3D.
    • Robot velocities: v,ωv, \omegav,ω, or wheel speeds, needed for dynamic models.
    • Actuator configuration: joint angles of an on-board arm, gripper state.
    • Locations and features of surrounding objects: landmarks, walls, doors; static unless they move.
    • Locations and velocities of moving objects and people.
    • Other variables: battery level, sensor health, whether a door is open.

    A state is complete if knowing it makes past states, measurements and controls irrelevant for predicting the future. This is the Markov assumption. It never holds exactly (unmodelled people, model errors), but filters work well when violations are small.

    Measurements, controls and their data streams

    ISA-1 question 2a asks for four terms. Write them precisely:

    • Sensor measurements are the information the robot obtains about its environment through its sensors; they reveal state only indirectly and noisily.
    • Control actions change the state of the world, most importantly the robot's own motion; even doing nothing lets the world change.
    • Measurement data: ztz_tzt​ is the measurement at time ttt; zt1:t2=zt1,zt1+1,…,zt2z_{t_1:t_2} = z_{t_1}, z_{t_1+1}, \ldots, z_{t_2}zt1​:t2​​=zt1​​,zt1​+1​,…,zt2​​ is the sequence.
    • Control data: utu_tut​ is the control that changes the state from xt−1x_{t-1}xt−1​ to xtx_txt​, for example a velocity command or an odometry reading.

    Measurements tend to decrease uncertainty (they bring information); controls tend to increase it (motion is noisy). A filter alternates the two.

    Probabilistic generative laws and the dynamic Bayes network

    Under the Markov assumption the evolution of state and measurements is governed by two conditional distributions:

    p(xt∣x0:t−1,z1:t−1,u1:t)=p(xt∣xt−1,ut)(state transition)p(x_t \mid x_{0:t-1}, z_{1:t-1}, u_{1:t}) = p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, u_t)\quad\text{(state transition)}p(xt​∣x0:t−1​,z1:t−1​,u1:t​)=p(xt​∣xt−1​,ut​)(state transition)
    p(zt∣x0:t,z1:t−1,u1:t)=p(zt∣xt)(measurement)p(z_t \mid x_{0:t}, z_{1:t-1}, u_{1:t}) = p(z_t \mid x_t)\quad\text{(measurement)}p(zt​∣x0:t​,z1:t−1​,u1:t​)=p(zt​∣xt​)(measurement)

    The belief after incorporating ztz_tzt​ is bel(xt)=p(xt∣z1:t,u1:t)bel(x_t) = p(x_t \mid z_{1:t}, u_{1:t})bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​); the prediction before incorporating it is bel‾(xt)=p(xt∣z1:t−1,u1:t)\overline{bel}(x_t) = p(x_t \mid z_{1:t-1}, u_{1:t})bel(xt​)=p(xt​∣z1:t−1​,u1:t​).

    3.2Bayes filter, representation and computation

    The Bayes filter algorithm

    The Bayes filter computes bel(xt)bel(x_t)bel(xt​) recursively from bel(xt−1)bel(x_{t-1})bel(xt−1​), the control utu_tut​ and the measurement ztz_tzt​. For every state xtx_txt​:

    bel‾(xt)=∫p(xt∣ut,xt−1) bel(xt−1) dxt−1(prediction)\overline{bel}(x_t) = \int p(x_t \mid u_t, x_{t-1})\,bel(x_{t-1})\,dx_{t-1}\qquad\text{(prediction)}bel(xt​)=∫p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1​)bel(xt−1​)dxt−1​(prediction)
    bel(xt)=η p(zt∣xt) bel‾(xt)(correction / measurement update)bel(x_t) = \eta\,p(z_t \mid x_t)\,\overline{bel}(x_t)\qquad\text{(correction / measurement update)}bel(xt​)=ηp(zt​∣xt​)bel(xt​)(correction / measurement update)

    Derivation sketch. Apply Bayes' rule to p(xt∣z1:t,u1:t)p(x_t \mid z_{1:t}, u_{1:t})p(xt​∣z1:t​,u1:t​) and use the measurement Markov assumption to reduce p(zt∣xt,z1:t−1,u1:t)p(z_t \mid x_t, z_{1:t-1}, u_{1:t})p(zt​∣xt​,z1:t−1​,u1:t​) to p(zt∣xt)p(z_t \mid x_t)p(zt​∣xt​); this gives the correction. Then expand bel‾(xt)\overline{bel}(x_t)bel(xt​) by total probability over xt−1x_{t-1}xt−1​ and use the state Markov assumption to reduce the transition to p(xt∣xt−1,ut)p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, u_t)p(xt​∣xt−1​,ut​); the remaining factor is bel(xt−1)bel(x_{t-1})bel(xt−1​), since future controls carry no information about past states.

    The door example: a Bayes filter you can compute by hand

    Representation and computation: the Bayes filter is not an algorithm yet

    For a continuous state the prediction integral and the correction have no closed form in general, so the Bayes filter is a framework. A concrete filter must choose how to represent the belief and how to compute the update. ISA-1 question 3b asks about the discrete Bayes filter and its use in continuous spaces: in a finite state space the integral becomes a sum and the filter is exact; for continuous spaces we either decompose the space into bins (histogram filter) or assume a parametric form (Gaussian filters) or use samples (particle filter).

    CriterionKalman / EKFHistogramParticle
    Belief shapeUnimodal GaussianAny, at bin resolutionAny, sample-based
    CostPolynomial in state dimensionExponential in dimensionLinear in particles
    Global localizationNoYesYes
    Nonlinear modelsApproximate (EKF)NaturalNatural

    3.3Gaussian filters

    Why Gaussians: closed-form updates

    Gaussian filters represent the belief by a mean μt\mu_tμt​ and covariance Σt\Sigma_tΣt​. The Gaussian family is closed under linear transformation and under multiplication by Gaussian likelihoods, so if the motion and measurement models are linear with additive Gaussian noise, the Bayes filter becomes exact matrix algebra. This is the moments parameterization (μ,Σ)(\mu, \Sigma)(μ,Σ); the canonical parameterization (ξ,Ω)(\xi, \Omega)(ξ,Ω) with Ω=Σ−1\Omega = \Sigma^{-1}Ω=Σ−1, ξ=Σ−1μ\xi = \Sigma^{-1}\muξ=Σ−1μ gives the information filter.

    The assumptions are the linear-Gaussian system:

    xt=Atxt−1+Btut+εt,εt∼N(0,Rt)x_t = A_t x_{t-1} + B_t u_t + \varepsilon_t,\quad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_t)xt​=At​xt−1​+Bt​ut​+εt​,εt​∼N(0,Rt​)
    zt=Ctxt+δt,δt∼N(0,Qt)z_t = C_t x_t + \delta_t,\quad \delta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_t)zt​=Ct​xt​+δt​,δt​∼N(0,Qt​)

    with a Gaussian initial belief. AAA is n×nn \times nn×n, BBB is n×mn \times mn×m, CCC is k×nk \times nk×n; RRR and QQQ are the process and measurement noise covariances.

    Limitation
    A Gaussian is unimodal. It cannot represent "I am at door 1 or door 3". Gaussian filters are therefore suited to position tracking, not global localization or the kidnapped-robot problem.

    3.4Kalman filter, extended Kalman filter, information filter

    The Kalman filter algorithm

    Algorithm Kalman_filter(μt−1,Σt−1,ut,zt)(\mu_{t-1}, \Sigma_{t-1}, u_t, z_t)(μt−1​,Σt−1​,ut​,zt​):

    μˉt=Atμt−1+Btut,Σˉt=AtΣt−1AtT+Rt\bar\mu_t = A_t\mu_{t-1} + B_t u_t,\qquad \bar\Sigma_t = A_t\Sigma_{t-1}A_t^T + R_tμˉ​t​=At​μt−1​+Bt​ut​,Σˉt​=At​Σt−1​AtT​+Rt​
    Kt=ΣˉtCtT (CtΣˉtCtT+Qt)−1K_t = \bar\Sigma_t C_t^T\,(C_t\bar\Sigma_t C_t^T + Q_t)^{-1}Kt​=Σˉt​CtT​(Ct​Σˉt​CtT​+Qt​)−1
    μt=μˉt+Kt(zt−Ctμˉt),Σt=(I−KtCt)Σˉt\mu_t = \bar\mu_t + K_t(z_t - C_t\bar\mu_t),\qquad \Sigma_t = (I - K_t C_t)\bar\Sigma_tμt​=μˉ​t​+Kt​(zt​−Ct​μˉ​t​),Σt​=(I−Kt​Ct​)Σˉt​

    The first line is the prediction; the rest is the correction. KtK_tKt​ is the Kalman gain: it weighs the innovation zt−Ctμˉtz_t - C_t\bar\mu_tzt​−Ct​μˉ​t​ by how uncertain the prediction is relative to the measurement. If the measurement is precise (Q→0Q \to 0Q→0) the gain approaches C−1C^{-1}C−1 and the filter trusts the sensor; if the prediction is precise (Σˉ→0\bar\Sigma \to 0Σˉ→0) the gain approaches 0 and the sensor is ignored.

    The extended Kalman filter: linearise, then apply Kalman

    Robot models are nonlinear: xt=g(ut,xt−1)+εtx_t = g(u_t, x_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_txt​=g(ut​,xt−1​)+εt​ (the motion contains cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ) and zt=h(xt)+δtz_t = h(x_t) + \delta_tzt​=h(xt​)+δt​ (range and bearing involve square roots and atan2). The EKF linearises both by a first-order Taylor expansion at the current mean, with Jacobians Gt=∂g/∂xt−1G_t = \partial g/\partial x_{t-1}Gt​=∂g/∂xt−1​ and Ht=∂h/∂xtH_t = \partial h/\partial x_tHt​=∂h/∂xt​:

    μˉt=g(ut,μt−1),Σˉt=GtΣt−1GtT+Rt\bar\mu_t = g(u_t, \mu_{t-1}),\quad \bar\Sigma_t = G_t\Sigma_{t-1}G_t^T + R_tμˉ​t​=g(ut​,μt−1​),Σˉt​=Gt​Σt−1​GtT​+Rt​
    Kt=ΣˉtHtT(HtΣˉtHtT+Qt)−1,μt=μˉt+Kt(zt−h(μˉt)),Σt=(I−KtHt)ΣˉtK_t = \bar\Sigma_t H_t^T(H_t\bar\Sigma_t H_t^T + Q_t)^{-1},\quad \mu_t = \bar\mu_t + K_t(z_t - h(\bar\mu_t)),\quad \Sigma_t = (I - K_t H_t)\bar\Sigma_tKt​=Σˉt​HtT​(Ht​Σˉt​HtT​+Qt​)−1,μt​=μˉ​t​+Kt​(zt​−h(μˉ​t​)),Σt​=(I−Kt​Ht​)Σˉt​
    When the EKF fails
    Linearisation error grows with nonlinearity and with uncertainty. A large heading uncertainty makes cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ strongly nonlinear over the belief, so the EKF can become overconfident and diverge. The unscented Kalman filter (UKF) propagates sigma points through ggg instead and is usually more accurate at similar cost.

    The information filter: the Kalman filter in canonical form

    ISA-1 question 3a asks you to derive an information filter. Replace (μ,Σ)(\mu, \Sigma)(μ,Σ) by the information matrix Ω=Σ−1\Omega = \Sigma^{-1}Ω=Σ−1 and information vector ξ=Σ−1μ\xi = \Sigma^{-1}\muξ=Σ−1μ. Substituting into the Kalman equations gives:

    Ωˉt=(AtΩt−1−1AtT+Rt)−1,ξˉt=Ωˉt(AtΩt−1−1ξt−1+Btut)\bar\Omega_t = (A_t\Omega_{t-1}^{-1}A_t^T + R_t)^{-1},\quad \bar\xi_t = \bar\Omega_t(A_t\Omega_{t-1}^{-1}\xi_{t-1} + B_t u_t)Ωˉt​=(At​Ωt−1−1​AtT​+Rt​)−1,ξˉ​t​=Ωˉt​(At​Ωt−1−1​ξt−1​+Bt​ut​)
    Ωt=CtTQt−1Ct+Ωˉt,ξt=CtTQt−1zt+ξˉt\Omega_t = C_t^T Q_t^{-1}C_t + \bar\Omega_t,\quad \xi_t = C_t^T Q_t^{-1}z_t + \bar\xi_tΩt​=CtT​Qt−1​Ct​+Ωˉt​,ξt​=CtT​Qt−1​zt​+ξˉ​t​

    Derivation of the correction: the exponent JtJ_tJt​ of the Kalman derivation expands to −12xT(CTQ−1C+Ωˉ)x+xT(CTQ−1z+ξˉ)-\tfrac12 x^T(C^TQ^{-1}C + \bar\Omega)x + x^T(C^TQ^{-1}z + \bar\xi)−21​xT(CTQ−1C+Ωˉ)x+xT(CTQ−1z+ξˉ​); reading off the quadratic and linear coefficients gives Ωt\Omega_tΩt​ and ξt\xi_tξt​ directly.     3.5Histogram filter, particle filter

    The histogram filter: decompose the state space

    The histogram filter divides the state space into finitely many regions xkx_{k}xk​ and keeps one probability pk,tp_{k,t}pk,t​ per region. The Bayes filter becomes the discrete Bayes filter:

    pˉk,t=∑ip(Xt=xk∣ut,Xt−1=xi) pi,t−1,pk,t=η p(zt∣Xt=xk) pˉk,t.\bar p_{k,t} = \sum_i p(X_t = x_k \mid u_t, X_{t-1} = x_i)\,p_{i,t-1},\qquad p_{k,t} = \eta\,p(z_t \mid X_t = x_k)\,\bar p_{k,t}.pˉ​k,t​=i∑​p(Xt​=xk​∣ut​,Xt−1​=xi​)pi,t−1​,pk,t​=ηp(zt​∣Xt​=xk​)pˉ​k,t​.

    It can represent any multimodal belief, at the cost of resolution and of memory that grows exponentially with state dimension. Static decomposition uses a fixed grid; dynamic decomposition (density trees) refines bins where probability is high.

    The particle filter: represent belief by samples

    The particle filter represents bel(xt)bel(x_t)bel(xt​) by a set of MMM samples Xt={xt[1],…,xt[M]}\mathcal{X}_t = \{x_t^{[1]}, \ldots, x_t^{[M]}\}Xt​={xt[1]​,…,xt[M]​}. Dense regions of particles mean high probability. Algorithm Particle_filter(Xt−1,ut,zt)(\mathcal{X}_{t-1}, u_t, z_t)(Xt−1​,ut​,zt​):

    1. For each particle mmm: sample xt[m]∼p(xt∣ut,xt−1[m])x_t^{[m]} \sim p(x_t \mid u_t, x_{t-1}^{[m]})xt[m]​∼p(xt​∣ut​,xt−1[m]​) (prediction by sampling the motion model).
    2. Compute the importance weight wt[m]=p(zt∣xt[m])w_t^{[m]} = p(z_t \mid x_t^{[m]})wt[m]​=p(zt​∣xt[m]​) (correction).
    3. Resample: draw MMM particles with replacement, with probability proportional to wt[m]w_t^{[m]}wt[m]​.

    Resampling turns the weighted set into an unweighted sample of the posterior; the low-variance (systematic) sampler uses one random number and MMM equally spaced pointers, costs O(M)O(M)O(M) and avoids losing diversity when all weights are equal.

    Worked particle weights and practical issues

    Four particles, one range reading
    1. Particles at x=1,2,3,4x = 1, 2, 3, 4x=1,2,3,4 m; measurement says z=2.6z = 2.6z=2.6 m with σ=0.5\sigma = 0.5σ=0.5 m.
    2. Unnormalised weights exp⁡(−12((z−x)/σ)2)\exp(-\tfrac12((z-x)/\sigma)^2)exp(−21​((z−x)/σ)2): 0.006, 0.487, 0.726, 0.020.
    3. Normalised: 0.005, 0.393, 0.586, 0.016.
    4. Weighted mean 2.61 m; effective sample size 1/∑w2=2.01/\sum w^2 = 2.01/∑w2=2.0: only two particles really count, so resampling is warranted.
    Pitfalls
    Particle deprivation: with too few particles none may lie near the true state, and the filter never recovers; add random particles (Chapter 6). Very accurate sensors make the likelihood so peaked that almost all weights vanish; inflate the sensor noise slightly. Resampling too often loses diversity; resample only when the effective sample size drops below about M/2.
    Real-life example
    The AMCL package used by default in ROS and Nav2 is a particle filter with KLD-adaptive particle counts, typically 500 to 5000 particles, running at 10-20 Hz on a single CPU core for warehouse and service robots.
