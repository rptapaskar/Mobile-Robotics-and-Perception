    1.1Intelligence and embodiment, a roboticist's problem, challenges of mobile autonomous robots

    What makes a robot mobile, and why that changes everything

    A stationary robot (an industrial arm bolted to a floor) works inside a fixed, known, fenced workspace. Its base frame never moves, so every joint angle maps to exactly one tool position, and its surroundings are engineered to be predictable. A mobile robot carries its own base through the world. It must answer three questions continuously: where am I, where am I going and how do I get there, and it must answer them with imperfect sensors in a space that was not designed for it.

    CriterionStationary manipulatorMobile robot
    ConstructionSerial chain on a fixed base; rigid, heavy, high-precision jointsChassis with a locomotion system, on-board power, sensing and compute
    WorkspaceBounded reach envelope, typically 1-3 mUnbounded in principle: a building, a warehouse, a field
    Position knowledgeExact from joint encoders (forward kinematics)Must be estimated; wheel odometry drifts
    EnvironmentStructured, fenced, staticSemi-structured or unstructured, shared with people
    Typical applicationsWelding, painting, pick-and-placeWarehouse logistics, cleaning, agriculture, inspection, planetary exploration

    This comparison answers the first part of TLO1 and is the frame for the whole course: mobility converts a geometry problem into an estimation problem.

    Intelligence needs a body: the embodied view of autonomy

    Early AI treated intelligence as symbol manipulation: plan in an abstract model, then execute. Mobile robots exposed the weakness of this view. A plan computed on a perfect map fails the moment a wheel slips or a person steps into the corridor. Embodiment is the idea that intelligent behaviour emerges from a closed loop between a physical body, its sensors and actuators, and the world. The robot's shape, wheel layout and sensor placement decide what it can perceive and do, and therefore what problems its software must solve.

    Rodney Brooks' subsumption architecture (1986) was the famous reaction: reactive layers wired directly from sensors to actuators, with no central world model. Modern robots combine both: a probabilistic world model (this course) with fast reactive safety layers underneath.

    A roboticist's problem: turning a task into sensing, estimation and control

    Correll frames the roboticist's problem as follows. Given a task (deliver a tote to station 4), a body and a set of sensors, design the software that closes the loop reliably. The task decomposes into four sub-problems that map directly onto this course:

    1. Locomotion and kinematics: how commanded wheel speeds become motion (Chapter 1, Chapter 4).
    2. Perception: how raw sensor data relate to the world (Chapter 5).
    3. State estimation: combining uncertain motion and uncertain perception into a belief about pose and map (Chapters 3, 6, 7).
    4. Planning: choosing actions that reach the goal safely (Chapter 2).

    Review question 4 uses the Ratslife game (two small e-puck robots compete in a maze to find feeders and recharge) to make this concrete. A trivial solution uses only short-range infrared proximity sensors and a wall-following rule: it never gets lost because it never needs to know where it is, but it wastes energy. A close-to-optimal solution adds wheel encoders for odometry, a camera to recognise the landmarks painted on the maze walls, and a map, so the robot can localize and plan the shortest route to the nearest feeder. The richer sensor suite buys efficiency at the cost of estimation software.

    The fundamental requirements every mobile robot must meet

    TLO3 asks you to discuss the fundamental requirements of a mobile robot. Examiners expect these, each with a one-line justification:

    • Locomotion: a mechanism matched to the terrain (wheels on floors, tracks on soil, legs on stairs).
    • Perception: proprioceptive sensors (encoders, IMU, battery monitor) measure the robot itself; exteroceptive sensors (LiDAR, camera, ultrasonic, bump) measure the world.
    • Localization: an estimate of pose in a reference frame, with its uncertainty.
    • Navigation and planning: global route planning plus local obstacle avoidance.
    • Energy autonomy: batteries sized for the duty cycle, and a docking or charging strategy.
    • Computation and communication: real-time on-board processing, fleet or operator links.
    • Safety: protective fields, emergency stop, speed limits near people (ISO 3691-4 for industrial trucks).
    Real-life example: warehouse AMRs in India
    Flipkart and Amazon India fulfilment centres use goods-to-person robots that carry shelving pods to pickers. Each robot meets every requirement above: differential drive on a flat floor, floor fiducials plus encoders for localization, a central planner for routes, automatic charging, and safety scanners. Removing any one requirement breaks the operation.

    Why mobile autonomy is hard: the five challenges

    TLO4 asks you to list and explain the challenges. Write them as follows:

    1. Sensor uncertainty: every sensor has noise, limited range and resolution, and can be fooled (glass walls to LiDAR, sunlight to infrared, symmetric corridors to any sensor).
    2. Actuation uncertainty: wheels slip, carpets compress, payloads shift the centre of mass; the commanded motion is never exactly executed.
    3. Unknown and dynamic environments: maps are incomplete or out of date; people, doors and pallets move.
    4. Resource limits: decisions must be made in tens of milliseconds on embedded computers with a finite battery.
    5. Safety and interaction: the robot shares space with people who behave unpredictably and must be protected by design.

    Which industries did robots transform, and in what order

    Review question 5 asks which industries robots entered first and which were revolutionized recently. Robots were introduced first in automotive manufacturing: the Unimate arm joined General Motors' die-casting line in 1961, followed by spot welding and painting in the 1970s and electronics assembly in the 1980s. These were all stationary robots in structured cells.

    The recent revolutions are driven by mobility and perception:

    • Warehousing and e-commerce logistics: Kiva Systems (acquired by Amazon in 2012) popularised goods-to-person AMRs; Amazon reports hundreds of thousands of drive units in service.
    • Agriculture: autonomous tractors with RTK-GNSS guidance, weeding and harvesting robots.
    • Healthcare and hospitality: hospital delivery robots, disinfection robots, restaurant service robots.
    • Domestic cleaning: robot vacuums, the largest-volume mobile robot product ever sold.
    • Inspection: quadrupeds such as Boston Dynamics Spot on oil and gas sites and power plants; drones for bridges and solar farms.
    Discuss
    For each industry above, which of the five challenges was the hardest to overcome, and what technology overcame it? (Hint: cheap LiDAR and GPU compute appear more than once.)

    1.2Locomotion, static and dynamic stability, degrees of freedom

    Locomotion: the ways a robot can move itself

    Locomotion is the process by which a robot moves its own body through the environment; it is distinct from manipulation, which moves objects. The choice of locomotion fixes the robot's mobility, efficiency and terrain capability.

    Mobility is the robot's ability to move in its environment; formally, the set of velocities it can achieve from a given configuration. A differential-drive robot has limited mobility (it cannot move sideways), whereas a mecanum-wheeled platform can move in any direction.

    Static versus dynamic stability

    A robot is statically stable if it remains at rest, without any control effort, when its motors are switched off. The condition is that the vertical projection of the centre of mass (CoM) lies inside the support polygon, the convex hull of the ground contact points. A robot is dynamically stable if it stays upright only through continuous active control, as in balancing or running.

    Review question 3. A car is statically stable: with four wheels the CoM projects inside the rectangle of contacts, and it stays put with the engine off. A Segway is dynamically stable: its two coaxial wheels give a support line with zero area, so any tilt produces a gravity torque that only the balancing controller can cancel; switched off, it falls. Three wheels are the minimum for static stability of a wheeled robot; two-wheeled robots need a caster or active balancing.

    Degrees of freedom, controllable DoF and holonomy

    The degrees of freedom (DoF) of a rigid body are the independent coordinates needed to describe its configuration: 6 in 3D space, 3 in the plane (x,y,θ)(x, y, \theta)(x,y,θ). For wheeled robots we distinguish:

    • DoF of the configuration: the dimension of the space of poses the robot can reach, normally 3 for a planar robot.
    • Differentiable or controllable DoF (DDoF): the number of independent velocities the robot can command at any instant.

    A robot is holonomic when DDoF equals DoF: it can move instantaneously in any direction of its configuration space. It is nonholonomic when DDoF is smaller than DoF because its constraints are on velocities, not positions. The classic nonholonomic constraint of a wheel that cannot slide sideways is

    x˙sin⁡θ−y˙cos⁡θ=0.\dot{x}\sin\theta - \dot{y}\cos\theta = 0 .x˙sinθ−y˙​cosθ=0.

    This forbids sideways velocity but does not forbid sideways positions: a car cannot slide into a parking space, yet it can reach it by manoeuvring. That is precisely what makes the constraint nonholonomic (not integrable into a position constraint).

    Wheel types determine the constraints

    Counting DoF for a wheeled robot is therefore a matter of counting independent sliding constraints. Each standard wheel on a common axle forbids the same sideways direction, so several wheels on one axle give only one independent constraint. Castors add support without adding constraints.

    Common mistake
    Do not count the wheel's own spin or the castor's swivel as degrees of freedom of the robot. The question is about the chassis pose (x,y,θ)(x, y, \theta)(x,y,θ); internal wheel angles are not part of it.

    Worked DoF answers: differential robot, office chair, car

    Review questions 1 and 2, and TLO2
    1. Differential-drive robot with one front castor. The chassis moves in the plane, so DoF = 3 (x,y,θ)(x, y, \theta)(x,y,θ). The two drive wheels share one axle and impose one sideways-slip constraint; the castor imposes none. Controllable DoF = 3 - 1 = 2 (forward speed vvv and turn rate ω\omegaω). It is nonholonomic, yet it can reach any pose by combining the two.
    2. Adding a second castor changes nothing kinematically: DoF stays 3, DDoF stays 2, because castors add no constraint. It improves static stability (four contacts instead of three) but on uneven floors one wheel may lose contact, so suspension becomes necessary.
    3. Office chair with all castor wheels. No wheel constrains the chassis, so it can translate in any direction and rotate: DoF = 3 and DDoF = 3. It is holonomic, which is exactly why a chair is easy to push sideways.
    4. Standard car (Chapter 2, TLO4). DoF = 3 in the plane; DDoF = 2 (speed and steering). The steering angle only changes the curvature of an arc about a common instantaneous centre; the car can still reach every point and heading by sequences of arcs, as in parallel parking.

    1.3Coordinate systems and frames of reference

    Why every robot quantity needs a frame

    A LiDAR reports that an obstacle is 2 m ahead. Ahead of what? The number is meaningful only in the sensor frame. To avoid the obstacle the planner needs it in the robot frame; to put it in a map it must be expressed in the world (map) frame. A frame of reference is an origin plus a set of orthonormal basis vectors; a coordinate system assigns numbers to points relative to that frame.

    ROS formalises this with the TF tree: map to odom to base_link to laser, each edge a transform published at its own rate.

    Forward kinematics of a differential-drive robot

    Forward kinematics maps actuator velocities to the chassis velocity.     Inverse kinematics: from desired motion to wheel speeds

    Inverse kinematics asks the opposite question: which wheel speeds produce a desired (v,ω)(v, \omega)(v,ω)? Inverting the two linear equations gives

    φ˙R=v+ωℓ/2r,φ˙L=v−ωℓ/2r.\dot\varphi_R = \frac{v + \omega\ell/2}{r},\qquad \dot\varphi_L = \frac{v - \omega\ell/2}{r}.φ˙​R​=rv+ωℓ/2​,φ˙​L​=rv−ωℓ/2​.

    The hard version of inverse kinematics is at the pose level: which wheel-speed history takes the robot to a goal pose (x∗,y∗,θ∗)(x^*, y^*, \theta^*)(x∗,y∗,θ∗)? The practical solution is to stop solving and start correcting: use feedback.

    Q1.3L2 UnderstandCO1TLO1PI 1.3.1Topics 1.210 marks

    Define mobility, locomotion, holonomy and degrees of freedom for a mobile robot. Explain with the example of a car why a nonholonomic robot can still reach every pose in the plane.

    Marking scheme
    MarksExpected answer content
    4Definitions, 1 mark each: locomotion (moving itself), mobility (set of achievable velocities), holonomic (controllable DoF equals DoF), DoF (independent configuration coordinates, 3 in the plane).
    2Nonholonomic constraint written: xdot sin(theta) - ydot cos(theta) = 0, constrains velocity not position (not integrable).
    4Car: DoF 3, DDoF 2; sideways velocity forbidden but sideways displacement achievable by sequences of arcs (parallel parking); hence every pose reachable.
